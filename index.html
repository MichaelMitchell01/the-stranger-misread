THE ARGUMENT

THREE READINGS
THAT MISS THE POINT.

Meursault is responsible for a killing. But the prosecution also puts his grief on trial. The two judgments are not the same.

01 THE PERSON

“He is incapable of feeling.”

THE EASY READING

LOOK CLOSER ↗

Meursault experiences physical sensation, attraction, and moments of connection. He does not perform grief in the familiar way others expect. Unusual expression is not proof of emotional emptiness.

02 THE TRIAL

“His trial is just about murder.”

THE EASY READING

LOOK CLOSER ↗

The prosecutor points to coffee and cigarettes at the vigil, then swimming and a comedy with Marie. He turns disapproved behavior into a story about criminal character.

03 THE PHILOSOPHY

“Absurdism excuses what he did.”

THE EASY READING

LOOK CLOSER ↗

Rejecting comforting explanations does not cancel moral responsibility. Camus's absurd is not an alibi for the killing or a reason to overlook the unnamed victim.

AUTHOR'S INTENT 1955 PREFACE

NOT FEELINGLESS.
UNWILLING TO PRETEND.

In his 1955 preface, Camus explained that Meursault refuses to exaggerate or invent feelings to satisfy other people. That challenges the idea that his quietness proves an absence of emotion. It does not amount to a clinical diagnosis or excuse the murder.

THE FUNERAL

Meursault doesn't mourn his mother in the way the people around him expect.

WHAT IT REVEALS

His behavior violates a social script. That tells us how others judge him; it does not establish whether he committed murder.

AN IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

A BAD TRIAL
DOESN'T MAKE
A MAN INNOCENT.

Meursault shoots an Arab man who is never personally named in the novel. In colonial Algeria, that absence is part of what readers must confront. The victim does not become less real because the trial focuses on Meursault's character.

This is not an argument for acquittal. It is an argument for examining the crime without confusing the defendant's failure to perform grief with proof of how or why he killed.

THE VERDICT

THE KILLING
IS REAL.
GRIEF IS NOT
THE PROOF.

Meursault is responsible for killing a man. Camus asks something harder than whether we like him: can we judge an action without treating nonconforming feelings as a second crime? And can we do that without forgetting the victim?
